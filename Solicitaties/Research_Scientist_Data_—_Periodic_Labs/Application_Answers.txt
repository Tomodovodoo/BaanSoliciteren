Tell us about a project you've worked on using LLMs or other AI tools.

For FirstProof Problem 9, I treated LLMs as fallible research workers. Separate agents searched for proof strategies, attacked assumptions, repaired arguments, and translated the result into Lean 4.

Model confidence never counted as evidence. Every accepted step had to compile in a pinned Lean/mathlib environment. Compiler failures became targeted feedback for the next attempt. The result was an informal proof plus a reproducible machine-checked formalization.

Please name 5 different mistakes generative AI has made in your interactions with it.

1. Invented a plausible Lean theorem or namespace that did not exist in the pinned mathlib version.

2. Quietly proved a weaker theorem by adding an assumption or changing a quantifier.

3. Claimed verification while an upstream dependency still contained `sorry`.

4. Guessed a simulator mechanic's timing or scope instead of marking it unknown.

5. Merged salts, tautomers, stereoisomers, or conflicting identifiers into one chemical record.

Please provide concrete examples of how LLMs can accelerate science in your domain of scientific expertise. Be as specific as possible.

In NMR prediction, agents can reconcile names, SMILES, InChI, salts, tautomers, and stereochemistry; extract solvent, field strength, temperature, and reference standards; then emit a provenance report for every transformation.

They can design controlled evaluations: scaffold versus random splits, per-functional-group errors, calibration, and architecture ablations. Scripts, RDKit, held-out data, and expert review remain the judges.

In formal mathematics, agents can search for lemmas, decompose proofs, translate arguments into Lean, and generate counterexample searches. Lean turns each attempt into an executable evaluation; failed goals and compiler errors become structured data for the next run.
